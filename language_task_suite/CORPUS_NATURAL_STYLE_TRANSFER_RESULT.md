# Natural spelling cues: existing bilinear checkpoints do not transfer

The [unedited FineWeb audit](CORPUS_NATURAL_STYLE_REFERENCE_RESULT.md) found a document-level US/UK spelling association. This experiment asks whether either existing depth-2, width-64 bilinear spelling checkpoint actually uses that earlier cue on **new natural prose**. The two [checkpoints](train_corpus_spelling_style_bilinear.py) were trained on source ranges around 53,500–83,499 with sampled document-wide US/UK transformations, not on this natural panel. They were both trained with 1,000× site weighting; one used paired US/UK training arms. Prior paired held-out tests already showed weak style transfer (both-arm two-way success below 1%), so this is a check of actual competence, not an assumption that the checkpoints learned the rule.

The [evaluator](eval_corpus_natural_style_transfer.py) scans 5,000 consecutive FineWeb `sample-10BT` documents at offsets 423,000–427,999 without selecting sources or changing their original text. It retains at most one cue/target pair per document, from different spelling families, with both words and the target's alternative spelling represented by compatible single BPE tokens in one ordinary 256-token chunk. This yields 286 valid pairs from 557 documents with a candidate spelling pair. In 259/286 pairs (90.6%), the earlier cue agrees with the observed later spelling. A global target-US majority gives 218/286 (76.2%); an **optimistic in-panel** target-family majority gives 223/286 (78.0%).

For each valid pair, the evaluator additionally changes **only the earlier cue** to its opposite US/UK spelling, retokenizes the document, and checks that the unchanged target remains the same BPE token and locally visible. The target's true counterfactual distribution is not known: this edit tests whether the model's US-versus-UK logit margin responds toward the new cue, not whether the original answer should deterministically flip. A synthetic length-changing `color`→`colour` fixture verified target-token alignment after the edit.

| Depth-2 checkpoint | Natural target correct in two-way choice | Full-vocabulary top-1 | Cue edit moves margin toward new cue | Mean signed cue response |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Paired US/UK training | 53.1% | 9.4% | 48.6% | −0.00007 logits |
| Original RMS-1000 training | 47.9% | 5.9% | 51.7% | −0.00472 logits |

Neither checkpoint approaches the observed target-family baseline, and neither shows directional cue sensitivity. The agreement-only subset gives the same conclusion; the 27 disagreeing examples are too few for a stable separate claim. The source-only edit is within natural English spelling vocabulary but may create stylistic inconsistency, so a positive response alone would still need a causal-validity audit. Here the near-zero response gives no support for a style circuit in these weights.

**Decision:** do not canonicalize or sparsify these checkpoints as if they contain a learned document-style mechanism. Keep natural spelling as a useful, realistic DGP hyperparameter and evaluation target, but train a model with stronger verified cue use before interpreting weight structure. The next training comparison should preserve full-token loss on a source-unconditioned reference stream, explicitly report any task-site oversampling, and evaluate whole-source and cue–target-family-combination holdouts. A sampled generative arm should control document style and surface variation while retaining naturally calibrated spelling frequencies; the corpus reference alone is not the requested toy-language DGP.

The [aggregate JSON](results/corpus_natural_style_transfer_start423000_n5000.json) includes source counts, target-family frequencies, agree/disagree strata, full-vocabulary and two-way prediction, and source-edit logit response for both checkpoints. A token-only paired panel cache remains local; no source prose or checkpoint was published.
